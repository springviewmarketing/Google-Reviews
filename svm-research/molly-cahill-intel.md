# Molly Cahill: competitive and market intelligence

Research compiled for Spring View Marketing, September 2026.

## Important correction to the brief

The brief described Molly Cahill as someone who "sells social media services to US eye care practices". That is not accurate, and it matters for how you read everything below.

Molly Cahill runs an Instagram marketing business for **US chiropractors, acupuncturists, functional medicine practitioners, massage therapists, physical therapists and myofascial release practitioners**. Her own about page describes serving "over 700 chiropractors, acupuncturists, functional medicine practitioners, and other local health professionals", and she states she actively manages around 28 chiropractic accounts. There is no eye care specialism anywhere on her site, in her podcast titles, or in her positioning.

I checked for an eye care angle specifically. There is none. Across all 180 episodes there is no optometry, optician, ophthalmology or eye care content of any kind. Episode 13 looked like a candidate, but its guests, Dr Nick Belden and Dr Hailey Koch, are chiropractors running a functional medicine and family chiropractic practice, not optometrists.

**Why I carried on anyway.** She is still an extremely close analogue to Spring View Marketing, arguably a better one than a US optical marketer would be:

- Solo-founder marketing service selling to independent, owner-operated local healthcare practices
- Same client profile: small following, dormant account, owner with no time and no marketing training
- Same commercial problem: justifying spend to an owner who sees marketing as a cost centre
- Chiropractic in the US is largely private-pay, like UK private dispensing and premium eyewear, so the conversion arguments transfer better than US vision-insurance-driven optometry would have

Where something is chiropractic-specific rather than healthcare-generic, I have said so. Every item is marked **TRANSFERABLE**, **US-ONLY** or **NEEDS ADAPTATION**.

## Sourcing and honesty notes

- **Episode index**: complete, all 180 episodes, built from the podcast RSS feed (dates and durations) cross-referenced with her site.
- **Transcripts**: her site publishes an "Episode Transcript" block on each episode page. I have not saved, copied or reproduced any transcript, per your copyright instruction. Everything below is extracted insight and paraphrase.
- **What I actually read**: her published show notes, which are substantial written write-ups of each episode averaging around 820 words, totalling roughly 144,000 words across 176 episodes. These are effectively a written version of each episode and are dense enough to mine properly.
- **Gaps**: 4 episodes (59, 106, 108, 125) have no show notes page on her site. Episode 89 has a page but it contains only a table of contents, no body. These are marked "description only" and I have not invented content for them.
- **Numbers**: every numeric claim is logged in Section E with an explicit flag for whether she names a source. Most do not have one.

# A. Owner objections

Every objection I could find a practice owner raising, grouped by theme, with her rebuttal. Owner phrasing is quoted where the wording is the point. Chiropractic and acupuncture owners, but the objections are the ones you will hear in a UK optician's back office almost word for word.

## A1. "I can't see the return"

**Objection: how do I know any of this is working?** The dominant objection across the whole show. (Ep 80, Ep 180)

Her rebuttal is the attribution-leakage argument, and it is the strongest commercial argument she makes. Social media influences bookings that get recorded as something else entirely. Her worked example: a friend refuses a chiropractic referral after seeing off-putting adjustment videos on TikTok, later relents, gets sent the practice's Instagram, sees an active and relevant account, feels reassured, and books. The intake form records that as "referred by a friend". The Instagram did the trust work and gets none of the credit. Same with Google: someone searches, lands on the website, clicks through to Instagram to have a look, then books, and records it as Google. **TRANSFERABLE**, and stronger in UK optics where "how did you hear about us?" is usually a single-choice field on a paper form.

She is also honest that she cannot attribute specific sales to her own podcast and justifies it as brand awareness. (Ep 80)

**Objection: I don't get patients from Instagram.** (Ep 171, quoted as one of the biggest myths she hears)

Her rebuttal reframes the purpose of the account entirely: people are often not using your Instagram to *discover* you, they are using it to *qualify* you. The silent questions a visitor is asking are: are you active, are you still in business, what do you specialise in, what does this practice feel like, do you seem trustworthy. Her supporting story is a hotel that lost a major food writer's visit because the account looked neglected. **This is the single most useful argument in the podcast for your target client**, because it turns a dormant account from a neutral into an active liability. **TRANSFERABLE**.

**Objection: my engagement is terrible, so it isn't working.** (Ep 154, Ep 160)

Two rebuttals. First, "silent engagement": educational posts, case studies and informational content drive bookings without generating comments, because people read, save and come back later. Second, engagement is not impact: people resonate and act without ever interacting publicly. She redirects to reach, profile visits, and real-world indicators such as new patient conversations and referrals. **TRANSFERABLE**.

## A2. "I don't have the time"

**Objection: "I just don't have time to film content."** (Ep 164, which she says is one of the most common things she hears)

Her diagnosis is that the problem is not ideas, it is timing. You are present with patients, and by the time you think about content the moment has passed. "Just remember to film" does not work. Her fixes are a permanent visual cue (a tripod left set up in the treatment room, five to eight second clips are enough) and a dedicated content day. **TRANSFERABLE**.

**Objection: "Ugh, I know I need to be more consistent on Instagram, but I just hate spending time on it."** (Ep 147)

**Objection: "If I learn to post consistently, won't that mean I'll spend MORE time on Instagram?"** She names this as the biggest fear practitioners raise. (Ep 147)

Her answer is boundaries rather than volume: create more than you consume, use an app blocker, take weekends off, do not open the app in the morning, turn off all notifications. She adds a genuinely non-obvious one: who you follow does not affect who sees your content, so mute colleagues who trigger comparison and deliberately train your Explore feed towards light content so the platform stops being demoralising. **TRANSFERABLE**.

**Objection: I'm too busy seeing patients.** (Ep 141, Ep 145, Ep 180)

She does not argue. She offers a bare-minimum tier instead: sort the bio, fix three pinned posts, post once a week including reused content, check DMs and comments twice a week. Framed as maintaining presence so patients feel confident you are open and available, explicitly not growth. **TRANSFERABLE**, and a useful model for a low-tier SVM package.

**Objection: I'm bad at time management, bad at technology, bad at business.** (Ep 173) Her counter: "more often than not, the problem isn't you. You just need some help."

## A3. "I don't want to do it / it isn't me"

**Objection: "Absolutely not, Molly. I am a private person."** (Ep 172)

Rebuttal: personal and private are not the same thing. Loving your dog, knitting or the beach is personal, not private. **TRANSFERABLE** and useful, because reserved UK optometrists will say exactly this.

**Objection: "But Molly, I'm not trying to be an influencer."** (Ep 172, Ep 122)

Rebuttal: you do not need to dance or overshare, you need to stop hiding behind generic content, because people with far less training than you are attracting attention simply by showing up as themselves. **TRANSFERABLE**.

**Objection: "I just can't get it quite right."** (Ep 85) Said by a student sitting on an outstanding case study she never published. Molly says this happens constantly. Rebuttal: social content is fleeting and "isn't that big of a deal"; move from "is this good enough?" to "let's try this out".

**Objection: "I'm not good at Canva."** (Ep 140) She treats this as a block standing in for something else, and the episode title is her answer: Canva is not a social media strategy.

**Objection: I need more training before I'm qualified to market myself.** (Ep 169) Rebuttal: confidence comes *after* visibility, not before it. "You learn by publishing. Not by endlessly preparing." **TRANSFERABLE**.

**Objection: I don't want to be salesy.** (Ep 131, Ep 147) Rebuttal is a diagnostic rather than a pep talk: if you avoid being salesy you are probably posting nothing but educational content and wondering why nobody books. "Your community needs clarity, not clues." **TRANSFERABLE**.

## A4. "My situation is different"

**Objection: "But I practice in a really small town."** (Ep 176)

Rebuttal: smaller communities usually have far less digital competition, so it is easier to become the practitioner people consistently see. Supported by a guest whose clinic sits in a town of around 7,000 people and draws patients from up to an hour away. (Ep 169) **TRANSFERABLE**, and directly usable for a market-town or Sheffield-suburb independent.

**Objection: my following is too small to bother.** (Ep 154)

Rebuttal, and the best line in the podcast for your client profile: "fewer followers can still lead to more bookings, because those followers are real people who can actually walk through your doors." She adds the vanity-metric counter: you could have 10,000 followers, but if none live within driving distance you will see nothing from it. (Ep 80) **TRANSFERABLE**.

**Objection: local Instagram is not enough / is a limitation.** (Ep 154) Rebuttal: local marketing is *easier* than online marketing because your town is a built-in niche. You do not need viral reach, you need to be recognisable and trusted by the right people.

**Objection: being specific will put people off.** (Ep 157, Ep 169) Rebuttal: "specificity doesn't repel people. It clarifies who you're for." Niching does not mean refusing everyone else; her guest's clinic is positioned on women's health but still treats far more. **TRANSFERABLE**, and the answer to an optician who fears that talking about dry eye means they look like they do not do routine sight tests.

## A5. "I already tried that"

**Objection on repurposing: "But I already posted that." / "But my audience has already seen it." / "But I already talked about that."** (Ep 171, and she reports "a lot of pushback" on this in Ep 165)

Rebuttal: most people have not seen it, not everyone sees everything, and repetition is how decisions get made. She reaches for effective-frequency numbers here, and those numbers do not survive scrutiny. See Section E. The *practice* of repurposing is well supported by her own operational behaviour; the *justifying statistic* is not.

**Objection: unfollows and unsubscribes mean I'm doing it wrong.** (Ep 167) Rebuttal: your content decisions stop being about helping your ideal patient and start being about avoiding rejection. Her own example is an email that produced 14 unsubscribes and 3 booked calls. **TRANSFERABLE**.

## A6. "Someone else can do it"

**Objection: my front desk person / daughter / a student can do this.** (Ep 145, Ep 180)

She does not dismiss it, which is worth noting because it is the objection that most threatens a paid service. She actively lists the free options: front desk team, a patient who loves social, a spouse or adult child, a college student. Her caveat is the useful part: "Okay, Sally at the front desk. You're in charge of social media now!" is not a plan. How many times should she post, what types of content, what is she responsible for daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, and how are you measuring it. **TRANSFERABLE**. The counter to this objection is not "they can't do it", it is "nobody has given them a spec".

**Objection: "But I don't have time to train someone."** (Ep 173) Rebuttal: record a Loom once, let AI turn the transcript into an SOP.

**Objection (unspoken): I can't let go of the account.** (Ep 67) She names this precisely. Owners stay tethered even after hiring help, because it is their face on Stories and their thinking in the captions. Her entire product ladder is built around this: the mid-tier product is explicitly sold as "a great way to outsource your social media marketing without relinquishing full control." Worth copying.

## A7. Cost

She addresses cost obliquely rather than head on, via time value. See Section D. The core move (Ep 67) is to convert owner hours into forgone clinical revenue: "Every hour you spend not with your patients or clients is an hour you're not generating revenue. If you're spending 5 hours a week coming up with content ideas, filming reels, and writing captions, that's hundreds to thousands of dollars you're giving up." **TRANSFERABLE and stronger in UK optics**, where chair time has an explicit price and a lost sight test slot is a knowable number.

# C. Content frameworks

## C1. The Content Ecosystem, her one named system (Ep 39, restated Ep 90, Ep 123, Ep 172)

This is the framework everything else hangs off. She is explicit that these are **themes, not pillars**: pillars are sub-topics that sit underneath themes.

A healthy ecosystem has three components: coverage of all five stages of customer awareness, a variety of media formats, and all six content themes.

**The six themes:**

| Theme | What it is | Needs original photography? |
|---|---|---|
| Education | Evergreen teaching: quick tips, tutorials, study overviews, research findings | No |
| Social proof | Testimonials, reviews, case studies, photos of you working, examples of problems you solve | Largely yes |
| Personal / lifestyle | Personality, interests, hobbies, food, family, pets | Yes |
| Direct pitch | Explicitly asks people to book or get in touch | No |
| Engaging | Exists purely to elicit comments: memes, quotes, questions | No |
| Collaboration / referral | Shouting out and tagging other businesses | No |

Themes deliberately overlap. An educational post can carry a direct pitch; a personal post can include a collaboration.

**The five stages of customer awareness** she attributes correctly to copywriter Eugene Schwartz: Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware, Product Aware, Most Aware. This is her one properly sourced framework. **TRANSFERABLE**.

**Media variety** is credited to Emily Schwalbach: some people want long-form detail, some want short captions, some want Reels and carousels, so mix formats deliberately.

**Her contrarian position, and the most important thing in this section.** She rejects the standard advice to simply post more of whatever performs best. Her reasoning: most people giving that advice monetise their content and are paid on views. A practice has a **conversion account**, not a monetisation account. The content that performs *worst* on engagement, direct pitches, is often what actually converts. So optimising purely for engagement actively harms a practice account. **TRANSFERABLE, and it is the intellectual backbone of selling to a 300 to 800 follower independent.**

## C2. The two definitions of "top performing" (Ep 165)

1. Posts that perform well on analytics.
2. Posts that lead to real results: bookings or patient conversations.

They sometimes overlap and sometimes do not. A post with very little engagement that brings in new patients is just as valuable. **This is the reporting frame I would adopt with clients**, because it inoculates you against the month where reach dips.

## C3. Cadence

Her numbers are consistent and are operating norms rather than researched benchmarks. All are her own agency's practice, self-reported.

- **Agency standard: 16 to 20 posts per month** for managed clinic accounts, of which **two or three are conversation starters** (Ep 160).
- **Her published package: 3 posts per week** (Presence Package, current site).
- **Growth mode for a DIY owner: roughly 4 posts per week / 16 a month** (Ep 180).
- **Bare minimum in a busy season: 1 post per week**, reused content counts (Ep 145).
- **Stories: daily**, and should take under 5 minutes (Ep 83).
- **Show your face at least every 6 posts** (Ep 109, heading only, reasoning not published).
- **Repost cycle for managed clients: every 90 days**, either identical or lightly tweaked (Ep 85). A guest suggests 6 months for her own account (Ep 46).
- **Professional photoshoot: once a year** (Ep 18), or **at minimum every 12 to 18 months** (Ep 164).
- **Give any new tactic four weeks before judging it** (Ep 179).
- **Review trigger: if reach has not grown for three months running, rethink the strategy** (Ep 80). This is the most useful single number she gives, because it is a decision rule rather than a benchmark.

## C4. Time required from the owner

She is unusually honest about this and the figures are consistent.

- **Growth mode: about 4 hours per week** to plan, create, post and engage (Ep 163).
- **Roughly 30 minutes per post** for her own experienced agency team, once the content plan and assets already exist. Some posts take ten minutes, an involved carousel can take an hour. At 16 posts a month that is about 8 hours, **and she states plainly that this excludes filming and gathering assets** (Ep 180).
- A client example: **two hours, twice a week** produces about 90% of their content, planned 2 to 3 weeks ahead, posting 3 times a week, pulling captions from a library (Ep 13).

**Read those together and the real number is the interesting one.** Thirty minutes per post assumes the assets exist. The asset-gathering time is excluded from every figure she gives. That excluded step is precisely what you sell.

## C5. Batching workflow (Ep 163)

Deliberately low tech. Plan one to two weeks at a time in a **Google Doc**. Every post gets an assigned role: education, engagement, or repurposed. Batch creation, but post natively rather than automating.

**She refuses to use Meta Business Suite autopost or scheduling automation.** Reasoning: Instagram is built for interaction, features do not translate when scheduled externally, native fonts, music and tools perform better, and "Instagram wants you using the app". The mechanism is unsourced but the position is a clear operational stance and a differentiator she markets on.

## C6. The content day (Ep 164, Ep 18, Ep 141)

Shot list for a dedicated session: short clips working with patients, different angles around the office, simple walkthrough shots, close-up clips of what you are doing.

The single best technique in the whole podcast for nervous practitioners: **have someone ask you questions while you work**, rather than asking you to perform to camera. It produces more comfortable and more usable footage. Add a cheap Bluetooth mic.

For patients: tell them in advance, expect some to decline being shown, and film from behind or focus on hands and movement instead. Store everything in one place, a shared album, Google Drive or a dedicated clinic phone.

**NEEDS ADAPTATION for the UK.** Her consent framing is a US social media release. In the UK you need explicit, informed, freely given consent under UK GDPR, recorded and withdrawable, plus GOC confidentiality standards. The faceless-patient technique transfers perfectly and is the practical answer for a nervous practice.

## C7. The repurposing mechanics (Ep 165)

Same video, different hook. Keep the hook and caption, change the background footage. Reel becomes carousel. Carousel becomes talking video. Reuse a proven hook across multiple posts. Change only the cover slide. Rule of thumb: when you batch, at least two posts should be repurposed versions of something already proven.

## C8. The escalation ladder (Ep 85)

A good workflow for turning one real event into several posts. Tell the story on Stories first as a disposable 24-hour first draft. Then make it a carousel case study. Then a face-to-camera Reel. By the time you record the Reel you have told the story three times and it sounds natural. You end up with three or four pieces on one topic and can see which resonates.

## C9. The 12-piece long-form engine (Ep 69, guest Jana Osofsky)

Create 12 strategic long-form pieces (blog posts, full YouTube episodes or podcast episodes) chosen by what people need to understand before they are ready to buy. Everything else is micro-content pulled from those 12.

**The alignment rule is the part worth keeping, and it applies directly to your blog offer:** every long-form piece must lead to a paid service. Her example: detailed educational blog posts about the importance of sleep will not get anyone booking chiropractic care. Tie sleep to chronic sleep issues that chiropractic addresses, and it is aligned. **Translated: a blog on screen time and eye strain only earns its place if it routes to a specific service, a dry eye assessment or a children's eye exam, not to general interest.**

## C10. Hook craft (Ep 24)

She trained in journalism and uses "don't bury the lede". Do not open with "Happy Tuesday!".

**Hook formula: [verb] + your [noun].** Her examples: "Ditch Your Sugar Cravings With These 3 Simple Tips", "Say goodbye to Your Neck Pain With This Easy Stretch", "Finally Get a Good Night's Sleep With This Nighttime Hack".

Also: cut dead air, start the clip as your mouth is opening to speak, be concise, always caption video.

## C11. The onboarding exercise (Ep 53, restated Ep 150)

This is what she does in week one of her paid programmes, and it is the most stealable process in the research.

1. List the last 20 to 25 patients you have seen.
2. Divide them ruthlessly into three groups: ideal patients you want more of; neutral ones; ones you hope will cancel.
3. For the ideal group only, write down the complaint that brought them in, what else they tried first, what result you are helping them achieve, and what you specifically did to get it.
4. Build the messaging from their words.

In Ep 150 she pairs this with reading real Google reviews, and in Ep 174 she adds the AI step: upload your reviews and instruct the model to "reflect back the language from my Google reviews when helping me write hooks and captions", specifically to avoid marketing jargon and AI fluff. **TRANSFERABLE and I would run this at every SVM onboarding.**

## C12. Her own list of the six mistakes (Ep 94 and 95, headings only)

1. Bio not optimised for local search
2. Not targeting a local audience
3. Page lacks personalisation
4. Only posting educational content
5. Forgetting about current patients
6. Not asking for help

Both episode pages publish only a table of contents, so I have the six headings and no supporting detail. Recorded here as her own framing, not as analysis.

# F. What she says does not work

## Failure modes she names

**Educational content on its own.** The most repeated point in the show. She calls it "the safety bubble" of educate, educate, educate (Ep 172), and lists "only posting educational content" as one of her six mistakes (Ep 95). Her diagnostic: if you avoid being salesy, you are probably posting nothing but education and wondering why nobody books (Ep 147). **TRANSFERABLE.**

**Generic content.** "3 reasons to see a chiropractor" is her recurring example of the post not worth making (Ep 166, Ep 169). The alternative is a specific scenario: "This baby was waking up eight times a night." **TRANSFERABLE, and the sharpest content lesson in the research.**

**Stock photos.** Named repeatedly. Ep 141's published heading is "Ditch the Stock Photos (They Don't Work Anymore)". In Ep 172 she describes the account that loses: "generic graphics, stock photos, and random holiday posts". **Commercially significant for you.**

**Pre-made Canva templates for Stories.** "Aren't effective and are, therefore, a waste of time" (Ep 40). Stories should be made in-app with native fonts.

**Meta Business Suite and autoposting** (Ep 163). Her stated reason is that Instagram is built for interaction and externally scheduled content feels less native.

**The "just checking in" email** (Ep 159). Fails because it guides the reader towards no specific action.

**Passive front-desk QR codes.** She explicitly rejects "putting a QR code at the front desk with a little 'Are you following us?' sign and calling it a day" in favour of actually asking patients (Ep 179).

**Handing social media to a staff member without a spec.** "Okay, Sally at the front desk. You're in charge of social media now!" is named as where practices go wrong (Ep 180).

**Chasing engagement metrics on a conversion account** (Ep 39). Optimising for what performs best pushes you away from direct pitches, which are what actually convert.

**Tracking unfollows and follower count** (Ep 167). "I never track my day-to-day follower count. It's not a metric that helps you grow."

**Waiting until content is perfect.** The Bell's Palsy case study that never got posted because "I just can't get it quite right" (Ep 85).

**Buying more information.** In Ep 170 she describes stitching a strategy together from saved Reels and free tutorials and getting more overwhelmed. "Sometimes the answer is not more information. It's a roadmap." Self-serving in context, but true.

**Commenting on smaller creators in your industry** to farm attention. She rules this out as inappropriate and limits the tactic to larger, non-competing accounts (Ep 44).

## Things she has changed her mind about

**Paid advertising.** She states plainly that she "always had a mental block around paid ads", had heard too many stories of expensive agencies and wasted money, was sceptical of the guest, and then changed her mind after speaking to him and checking with chiropractors she trusts (Ep 176). Worth noting she is not independent in that episode either, but she does disclose.

**Production values.** The clearest and most important shift, and it cuts against your differentiator, so take it seriously. In Ep 171: "A few years ago, brands were spending thousands of dollars on professional photo shoots and highly produced video content. Now? Some of the most effective content is the exact opposite." Native content that feels natural to the platform often outperforms polished content. Ep 128's headings say the same: casual content converts better now.

**How to read that tension.** She has not abandoned photography. Her agency still *requires* clients to supply professionally shot brand photos (Ep 40), she still recommends an annual shoot (Ep 18), and she still tells practitioners to plan a content day (Ep 164). What she has abandoned is the *advert aesthetic*. So the position that survives all of it is: **real, specific, in-practice imagery beats both stock graphics and glossy commercial photography.** That is a narrower differentiator than "professional photography" but it is a more defensible one, and it happens to be exactly what a photographer embedded in the practice can deliver and a remote agency cannot.

**Repurposing.** She reports having to argue against her own audience's instinct that reuse is cheating (Ep 165, Ep 171), and her practice has hardened into a 90-day repost cycle for managed clients.

# G. Portable post formats

Every reusable post idea she recommends that could run across multiple practices while still driving genuine local engagement. **Sorted by how well each survives translation to a UK independent optician with 300 to 800 followers.**

For each: the skeleton, what must be swapped per practice, what it asks the audience to do, any engagement result she claims, whether it needs original photography, and how often it can repeat before wearing out.

Where she gives no wear-out guidance, I have applied her stated general standards: a 90-day repost cycle for managed clients (Ep 85) and permission to reuse proven frameworks indefinitely with the variables changed (Ep 178).

### 1. "Patients on our schedule this week in [town]" (Ep 157)

- **Skeleton:** 5 to 7 seconds of office B-roll, text overlay naming your town, then 3 to 4 types of patient you are genuinely seeing this week.
- **Swap per practice:** town name, the actual patient types, your own office footage.
- **Asks the audience to:** recognise themselves. No explicit action. She is explicit the power "isn't in the views, it's in recognition" and the internal reaction is "Oh, they see people like me."
- **Claimed result:** none, no number. She claims it builds specificity, strengthens local keywords and quietly builds trust.
- **Photography:** **Required.** Short B-roll of your own premises. Collapses entirely without it.
- **Repeat:** she says explicitly "you can do it over and over again with different cases". Weekly is sustainable because the content changes with the week.
- **UK verdict: the strongest format in the list.** Optics has unusually legible patient types: first varifocals, a child's first glasses, a screen-heavy office worker, someone finally sorting dry eye, a returning contact lens wearer. **TRANSFERABLE.** Keep it non-identifying and it raises no GOC or GDPR issue.

### 2. "If you're a [person] in [town] who wants [outcome], you're in the right place" (Ep 157, Ep 178)

- **Skeleton:** same short B-roll, one sentence of text naming person, place and outcome.
- **Swap per practice:** the three variables, plus your own footage.
- **Asks the audience to:** self-identify, and implicitly to get in touch.
- **Claimed result:** none, no number. Rationale given is that Instagram functions as a search engine and specificity improves discoverability.
- **Photography:** **Required**, though a single reusable clip can carry many versions.
- **Repeat:** she says "once you create the structure once, you can reuse it forever" with different variables. Practically, monthly with a fresh combination.
- **UK verdict: excellent.** "If you're a parent in Hillsborough worried your child is squinting at the whiteboard, you're in the right place." **TRANSFERABLE**, with one caution: keep the outcome a service description rather than a treatment promise, or you are into ASA/CAP territory.

### 3. Conversation starter, her "can't help myself" post (Ep 160, Ep 44)

- **Skeleton:** one question, answerable in about three seconds, specific to your audience, often with a comment key such as a coloured heart per answer range.
- **Swap per practice:** the question, tuned to your actual patient base.
- **Asks the audience to:** comment. Explicitly the point of the format.
- **Claimed result:** no number. Her mechanism claim is that comments signal to the algorithm that your content is worth showing, which then helps your educational and case-study posts reach further.
- **Photography:** **Not required.** Runs on graphics alone.
- **Repeat:** she gives a hard ratio: **2 to 3 per month out of 16 to 20 posts.**
- **UK verdict: strong, with a caveat.** "How old were you when you got your first pair of glasses?" or "Be honest, how long have you been putting off your eye test?" are genuinely good. Her rules matter: avoid generic health questions like "How are you feeling?", avoid clinical jargon. **TRANSFERABLE.** Watch the line between a conversation starter and engagement bait, see the avoid list below.

### 4. Weekly availability post (Stories, and as an email) (Ep 170, Ep 83)

- **Skeleton:** a photo you already have, plus typed text listing this week's open appointment times, linked to booking. She is emphatic: do not open Canva.
- **Swap per practice:** the times, the photo, the booking link.
- **Asks the audience to:** book, now.
- **Claimed result:** "most often filling existing patient spots", sometimes new patients. **No number.** Her supporting anecdote is that she books most of her own massages from her chiropractor's last-minute openings posts.
- **Photography:** **Not required**, and she actively argues against making it precious. Any existing photo works.
- **Repeat:** weekly, indefinitely. The mechanism depends on repetition, training people to check.
- **UK verdict: the highest-conversion, lowest-effort item in the research.** Its dependency is the blocker: it needs online booking, or at minimum a click-to-call. Many UK independents do not have it. **TRANSFERABLE**, and the accompanying email version sits comfortably under PECR soft opt-in for existing patients provided every send carries an opt-out.

### 5. Local collaboration post: "Our recommendation for [category]" (Ep 84)

- **Skeleton:** a carousel recommending local providers, published as an Instagram *collaboration* so it lands on their grid too. Up to 5 collaborators per post.
- **Swap per practice:** the category, the local businesses, the town.
- **Asks the audience to:** discover the collaborator, and implicitly to trust you by association. The real ask is of the collaborator, to accept.
- **Claimed result:** for a variant where a client's interview was cut into 4 collaboration posts, she claims "so much visibility". **No number.**
- **Photography:** **Not required**, and she actively recommends **neutral, low-branded images** so the post does not clash with the collaborator's grid. You can even build it in their brand colours.
- **Repeat:** monthly, with different partners. She recommends multiple posts with the same partner to compound reach.
- **UK verdict: very strong and badly under-used by UK independents.** Craft notes that make or break it: tag is not collaboration, a tag lands in a hidden tab nobody opens; always DM the partner to tell them the request is waiting, because most people do not know the feature exists; target local, because strangers decline. **TRANSFERABLE.**

### 6. Patient repost campaign (Ep 179, Ep 170)

- **Skeleton:** not a post but a campaign wrapped around your posts. For four weeks, ask one or two patients per clinic day to hit repost, comment or follow. Script provided: "We're really trying to reach more people in the community. Would you mind following us and leaving a couple comments?"
- **Swap per practice:** nothing except the ask.
- **Asks the audience to:** repost, comment, follow.
- **Claimed result:** her modelled arithmetic, **which she caveats herself**: 8 patients a week at roughly 200 local followers each is up to 1,600 potential views a week and 6,400 over four weeks, but the algorithm decides who actually sees it. Separately she reports a cohort practice going from 5,000 to over 40,000 monthly views, with an office manager reporting "an influx of appointment requests".
- **Photography:** **Not required**, it amplifies whatever you already post.
- **Repeat:** run as a four-week campaign, then rest. She insists on four weeks before judging.
- **UK verdict: strong, free, and the fastest route to local reach for a 300 to 800 follower account.** **TRANSFERABLE**, with one adaptation: she floats incentives "depending on what's allowed in your state". **Do not incentivise in the UK.** Asking is fine. Rewarding patients for promotion is not, once you factor in GOC standards on patient relationships and ASA/CAP rules on unidentified marketing.

### 7. Meet the team / personal content (Ep 172, Ep 145)

- **Skeleton:** a Reel or carousel introducing a person, either quick fun facts or identity plus expertise, for example "I'm a [age] mum of two and an optometrist, and here are the only [category] worth spending money on."
- **Swap per practice:** the person, their actual traits, their genuine opinions.
- **Asks the audience to:** get to know you. Often paired with a light question.
- **Claimed result:** she says practitioners who add personal content consistently "tend to see better results", drawn from managing dozens of accounts. **No number, observational.**
- **Photography:** **Required.** This is the format that most obviously dies on stock imagery.
- **Repeat:** monthly per person. A multi-optometrist practice can rotate staff and extend the runway considerably.
- **UK verdict: strong, and the one your competitor structurally cannot serve well.** Her private-versus-personal distinction is the tool for talking a reserved UK optometrist into it. **TRANSFERABLE.**

### 8. Case study carousel (Ep 85, Ep 109, Ep 39)

- **Skeleton:** one real patient's situation: what they came in with, what they had tried, what you did, what changed.
- **Swap per practice:** the case, entirely.
- **Asks the audience to:** recognise their own situation, and get in touch.
- **Claimed result:** none specific. Positioned inside the social proof theme.
- **Photography:** **Strongly preferred.** Works better with real imagery than with a template, though a well-designed carousel can carry it.
- **Repeat:** monthly, with the escalation ladder applied: Story first, then carousel, then Reel.
- **UK verdict: high value, highest compliance load.** **NEEDS ADAPTATION.** Requires explicit written patient consent under UK GDPR, and any clinical claim must be capable of substantiation under CAP. Anonymised and outcome-modest, it is one of the best posts an optician can make. Done carelessly it is the one that gets a practice in trouble.

### 9. Five-star review turned into a Reel (Ep 109, heading only, plus Ep 174)

- **Skeleton:** a real review on screen, read aloud or captioned, over practice B-roll.
- **Swap per practice:** the review.
- **Asks the audience to:** trust, and often to leave their own review.
- **Claimed result:** none. Ep 109's page publishes headings only, so I have the format name and no supporting detail.
- **Photography:** **Preferred**, since it needs a backdrop, but graphics alone will do.
- **Repeat:** every few weeks, as reviews arrive.
- **UK verdict: good and low risk, with one hard rule.** **NEEDS ADAPTATION.** Reviews must be genuine, unincentivised and unedited in substance. Do not gate review requests to happy patients only. CMA rules on fake and incentivised reviews now carry real penalties, and this is an area where UK enforcement is tighter than US practice.

### 10. "Challenge a common assumption" Reel (Ep 178)

- **Skeleton:** hook that questions an existing belief, then a specific number of alternatives.
- **Swap per practice:** the belief and the alternatives.
- **Asks the audience to:** keep watching, and reconsider.
- **Claimed result:** none. Described as evergreen and reusable for months or years.
- **Photography:** **Not required**, though B-roll improves it.
- **Repeat:** she frames these as permanent rotation items. Monthly with a different assumption.
- **UK verdict: good, handle claims carefully.** "Think you need to wait two years between eye tests?" works. **NEEDS ADAPTATION** wherever the assumption being challenged touches clinical advice: CAP requires substantiation, and GOC standards expect you not to mislead about clinical matters.

### 11. "A little birdie told me you're looking for [thing]" (Ep 157)

- **Skeleton:** short B-roll, one line of text, nothing else. No script.
- **Swap per practice:** the thing.
- **Asks the audience to:** feel seen and respond.
- **Claimed result:** none.
- **Photography:** **Required**, though it reuses the same clip library as formats 1 and 2.
- **Repeat:** monthly.
- **UK verdict: works, but the phrasing is very American.** **NEEDS ADAPTATION** on tone rather than compliance. A UK version needs drier wording.

### 12. "After [N] years in practice, here's what I don't agree with" (Ep 178)

- **Skeleton:** open with tenure, name a common belief you disagree with, explain why.
- **Swap per practice:** the years, the belief.
- **Asks the audience to:** hear your philosophy.
- **Claimed result:** none.
- **Photography:** face to camera, so **required**.
- **Repeat:** every couple of months.
- **UK verdict: good for an established independent, where longevity is the asset.** She is explicit it must not read as criticising other providers. **NEEDS ADAPTATION**: in the UK, disparaging identifiable competitors, including the multiples, risks both CAP comparative advertising rules and GOC professionalism standards. Keep it about your philosophy, never about Specsavers.

### 13. Poll with a "just here to see the results" option (Ep 83)

- **Skeleton:** a Stories poll framed so people feel comfortable voting, always including a low-commitment option.
- **Swap per practice:** the question.
- **Asks the audience to:** vote.
- **Claimed result:** none.
- **Photography:** **Not required.**
- **Repeat:** weekly.
- **UK verdict: fine, low ceiling.** Useful for keeping a Story habit alive. **TRANSFERABLE.**

### 14. Tag a local business from out and about (Ep 83, Ep 162)

- **Skeleton:** a Story from a local coffee shop, barber or shop, tagging them.
- **Swap per practice:** the business.
- **Asks the audience to:** nothing directly. The ask is that the business reposts you.
- **Claimed result:** none, but the rationale is sound: local businesses are more likely to reshare than large accounts. Paired with her "spend 15 minutes engaging with other local businesses" habit.
- **Photography:** a phone snap, so **not required** in any real sense.
- **Repeat:** weekly.
- **UK verdict: genuinely effective and almost free.** **TRANSFERABLE.** This is community engagement rather than content, and note it is a $375/month add-on in her own pricing.

### 15. "Micro habits I wish all my patients would adopt" carousel (Ep 172)

- **Skeleton:** a numbered carousel of mostly free habits, framed through your own perspective.
- **Swap per practice:** the habits, and the practitioner's voice.
- **Asks the audience to:** save, and often to comment which one they will try.
- **Claimed result:** none.
- **Photography:** **Not required.**
- **Repeat:** quarterly.
- **UK verdict: safe, useful, unremarkable.** The optics version writes itself, though it is closer to filler than to community engagement. **TRANSFERABLE**, provided habit claims stay modest and evidence-backed.

### 16. Office tour or "meet the team" as a pinned post (Ep 145)

- **Skeleton:** a Reel touring the practice or introducing the team, pinned to the profile permanently.
- **Swap per practice:** everything.
- **Asks the audience to:** orient themselves, and book.
- **Claimed result:** she claims three good pinned posts "carry 90% of the weight" in a busy season. That figure is a figure of speech, see Section E.
- **Photography:** **Required.**
- **Repeat:** replaced annually rather than repeated. This is infrastructure, not a post.
- **UK verdict: high value, and under-used.** Add parking and location information, which she specifically recommends for brick and mortar. **TRANSFERABLE.**

### 17. "Just had this conversation" video (Ep 109, heading only; technique described Ep 85)

- **Skeleton:** face to camera straight after an appointment, talking about something that just came up.
- **Swap per practice:** the conversation.
- **Asks the audience to:** relate.
- **Claimed result:** none available. Ep 109 publishes headings only.
- **Photography:** self-filmed, so **not required** in any equipment sense, but it is original footage.
- **Repeat:** weekly if the owner will do it, which is the real constraint.
- **UK verdict: good, and the hardest to get a reserved UK optometrist to actually do.** **NEEDS ADAPTATION**: nothing identifying, ever. Her car-filming trick (parked, good light, quiet, nobody watching) is the practical unlock.

### 18. Keyword comment to unlock a link (Ep 44)

- **Skeleton:** instead of "link in bio", ask people to comment a keyword, then DM them the link.
- **Swap per practice:** the keyword and the resource.
- **Asks the audience to:** comment a specific word.
- **Claimed result:** no number. Mechanism claim: DM conversations signal to the algorithm.
- **Photography:** **Not required.**
- **Repeat:** whenever you have something worth gating.
- **UK verdict: works, but it needs something worth downloading**, which most opticians do not yet have. Lower priority until there is a lead magnet. **TRANSFERABLE**, but note that DMing a link and then adding that person to an email list requires a lawful basis and clear consent under UK GDPR and PECR.

### 19. "Instead of [common remedy], may I suggest..." (Ep 178)

- **Skeleton:** compare a common recommendation with your own approach.
- **Swap per practice:** the remedy and the alternative.
- **Asks the audience to:** reconsider.
- **Claimed result:** none.
- **Photography:** **Not required.**
- **Repeat:** occasionally.
- **UK verdict: the riskiest format in the list and I would not lead with it.** Her own example is "Instead of ibuprofen for headaches, may I suggest..." For a GOC registrant, positioning a service against a medicine or an established clinical pathway is a substantiation problem under CAP and a professionalism problem under GOC standards. **NEEDS ADAPTATION**, and honestly it is easier to just use format 10 instead.

## G1. Formats that collapse into generic filler without real photography

This is the commercially interesting list, because photography is your differentiator and your main competitor requires clients to supply their own assets. Molly's agency has exactly the same requirement (Ep 40): clients "provide us with the videos as well as brand photos taken by a professional photographer".

**Cannot run at all without original imagery:**

1. **"Patients on our schedule this week in [town]"** (1) - the office B-roll *is* the format. Without it you have a text graphic listing patient types, which reads as an advert.
2. **Meet the team and personal content** (7) - the entire point is showing the human. On stock imagery it is worse than not posting, because it looks like a chain pretending to be local.
3. **Office tour and pinned posts** (16) - literally impossible without footage of the actual practice.
4. **"You're in the right place"** (2) and **"A little birdie told me"** (11) - both are one line of text over real premises. Over a stock photo they become the exact generic content she spends the podcast attacking.

**Badly degraded without it:**

5. **Case study carousels** (8) - survive as design, but real imagery of the practice, the frames, the equipment is what converts them from a template into proof.
6. **Review-to-Reel** (9) - needs a real backdrop or it looks like every other review graphic.
7. **"After N years in practice"** (12) and **"Just had this conversation"** (17) - face-to-camera, so they need the owner filmed, which is the thing she says owners never get round to.

**The commercial reading.** Nine of the nineteen formats, including her three strongest local-recognition formats, are photography-dependent. Her own agency does not supply photography, does not shoot video, and charges $375/month extra even to comment on local businesses. A UK practice following her model has to find a brand photographer, vet them for indoor and flash competence (her own advice, Ep 18, which tells you it is a real skill gap), pay $500 to $1,000, and then still gather B-roll themselves between patients.

**That gap is your offer.** Not "we also do photography", but: the formats that actually build local recognition cannot be produced remotely, and everyone selling this service remotely is quietly requiring the client to solve the hardest part themselves.

**The honest counter-argument, which you should be ready for.** She now says polished production can underperform native, casual content (Ep 171, Ep 128). That is real and you should not pretend otherwise. The defensible position is not "better photography wins". It is: **specific, real, in-practice imagery beats both stock graphics and glossy advert-style photography, and it has to be captured on site by someone who knows what to look for.** Sell the asset library and the capture habit, not the gear.

## G2. Formats to avoid, or to change before using in the UK

**Giveaways (Ep 44).** She recommends a giveaway restricted to current patients, pinned to the profile, promoted by email, ideally with a partner business. **Do not port this directly.**
- UK prize promotions must comply with CAP Section 8: significant conditions stated up front, closing date, how winners are selected and notified, promoter details.
- More seriously, a giveaway aimed at *current patients* of a healthcare provider edges towards inducement. Combined with the GOC's standards on maintaining proper professional relationships, this is not worth the risk for the reach it buys.
- If a practice insists, run it as a general local prize draw with no purchase, no booking and no clinical link, and keep it away from anything that looks like rewarding patients for treatment.

**Incentivised patient advocacy (Ep 179).** She suggests "creating incentives around sharing posts or referring friends", hedged with "depending on what's allowed in your state". **Do not do this in the UK.** Paid or rewarded promotion by patients is undisclosed marketing under CAP unless clearly labelled, and it is an obvious professional problem for a GOC registrant. **Asking patients to follow, comment or repost is fine and is the version to use.**

**Anything touching reviews and incentives (Ep 44, Ep 162).** "Sometimes it's as simple as asking" is correct and fine. Rewarding reviews, filtering who gets asked, or editing what they say is not. UK enforcement here is now materially stricter than US practice under the DMCC regime.

**Influencer partnerships (Ep 13, Ep 171).** Reaching out to "local celebrities or influencers" is recommended without much caution. In the UK any gifted or paid arrangement requires prominent, unambiguous disclosure, and a healthcare provider making clinical claims through a third party carries the substantiation burden itself. **NEEDS ADAPTATION**, not a ban.

**"Instead of [medicine], may I suggest..." (Ep 178).** Covered above at format 19. The clearest substantiation and professionalism risk in the set.

**Engagement bait specifically.** She is mostly careful here, and her rules are actually the right ones: the question must be genuinely easy and relevant, not "comment to win" or "tag three friends". But two of her tactics sit close to the line and should be used with judgement rather than as policy:
- **Asking existing patients to comment to "inject new life" into flat content** (Ep 44). Manufactured engagement. Harmless at small scale, but it is optimising the metric rather than the outcome, which is the exact error she criticises elsewhere.
- **Commenting on larger creators' posts to borrow attention** (Ep 44). She sets a sensible boundary, larger and non-competing only, but for a local optician the return is close to zero. Fifteen minutes on local business accounts (Ep 162) is a far better use of the same time.

**Not in her material, but worth stating since these are the classic traps:** follow-for-follow, follower buying, and comment pods appear nowhere in her advice. To her credit, she argues directly against follower-count thinking (Ep 167). Do not let them creep into an SVM offer either.

## G3. US-only or structurally different, across the whole section

Little of the *format* material is genuinely US-only, which is why she is such a useful analogue. What differs is context rather than tactics:

- **"Depending on what's allowed in your state"** (Ep 179) is state-level scope-of-practice and inducement regulation. The UK equivalents are GOC standards, ASA and CAP, and the CMA on reviews. Broadly stricter, and centrally enforced.
- **Patient photography consent** is framed as a social media release (Ep 18). UK GDPR needs more: explicit, informed, freely given, recorded, and withdrawable.
- **Email to patient databases** (Ep 170, Ep 179, Ep 159) is treated as unproblematic. In the UK it runs on PECR soft opt-in for existing patients, needs an opt-out in every message, and needs care where the content shades from service message into marketing.
- **"Don't hire outside of the U.S."** (Ep 40) is her security and algorithm argument for hiring domestically. The logic inverts neatly in your favour: a UK practice should want a UK-based provider who knows the town.
- **Vision insurance, HIPAA, OD titles and US chains** do not appear anywhere in her material, because she does not serve eye care. There is nothing to translate. The UK-specific layer you need to add yourself is NHS sight tests and vouchers, GOC registration, and the competitive reality of Specsavers and Vision Express rather than US optical chains.
- **US holidays** appear only lightly, mostly the Black Friday and Cyber Monday paid-ads squeeze on organic reach (Ep 44), which now applies in the UK too.
- **Franchise structures and FTC review rules** do not feature in her material at all.

# What I'd actually use

## Ten strategic and commercial takeaways

1. **Sell the dormant account as a liability, not a missed opportunity** - people use a practice's Instagram to *qualify* it after finding you elsewhere, so a neglected account actively loses bookings rather than merely failing to win them (Ep 171).
2. **Price against forgone chair time, never against other agencies** - an hour the owner spends in Canva is an hour not testing or dispensing, and in optics that hour has a knowable price, which is a stronger argument than any package comparison (Ep 67).
3. **Photography is the structural gap in the competitor model, not a nice-to-have** - Molly's agency, like your main competitor, requires clients to source and pay a professional photographer themselves, and nine of her nineteen post formats collapse without original imagery (Ep 40, Ep 18).
4. **Position on real in-practice imagery rather than production quality**, because she now argues polished advert-style content underperforms native content, and the defensible claim is specificity and access, not gear (Ep 171, Ep 128).
5. **Build the product ladder around the control objection, not the price objection** - her self-serve tier is explicitly sold as outsourcing "without relinquishing full control", which is what actually stops owners buying (Ep 67).
6. **Open every engagement with the patient-language harvest**, listing the last 20 to 25 patients, sorting them ruthlessly into ideal, neutral and hope-they-cancel, and building all messaging from what the ideal group actually said (Ep 53, Ep 150, Ep 174).
7. **Include DM and comment handling in the base retainer**, because she explicitly excludes it and charges $375 a month for a thinner version, and it is the part a local practice most visibly fails at.
8. **Sell a minimum term on the "boring middle" argument** rather than a contractual one - most strategies fail because they were not given long enough, and her own standard is four weeks before judging any tactic (Ep 150, Ep 179).
9. **Lead the audit with lead tracking, not content** - counting enquiries, who answered the phone, how many booked and how many attended is cheap, credible, and almost no UK independent does it (Ep 151).
10. **Do not repeat her statistics** - she gives four different unsourced numbers for effective frequency across four episodes, and her headline result claims come from vendors selling the thing being measured; the tactics survive scrutiny, the numbers do not (Section E).

## Fifteen portable post formats, ranked for a UK independent with 300 to 800 followers

Ranked by how much genuine community engagement each would drive at that follower count.

1. **"Patients on our schedule this week in [town]"** over 5 seconds of real practice B-roll, listing 3 to 4 genuine patient types.
2. **Weekly appointment availability** on Stories and by email, same day each week, linked straight to booking.
3. **Four-week patient repost campaign** - ask one or two patients per clinic day to repost, comment or follow, with no incentive attached.
4. **Local collaboration carousel**, "Our recommendations for [town]", published as an Instagram collaboration with up to five local businesses, in neutral colours, with a DM to each.
5. **"If you're a [person] in [town] who wants [outcome], you're in the right place"**, over the same reusable B-roll library.
6. **Conversation starter**, one question answerable in three seconds and specific to eyes, capped at two or three a month.
7. **Meet the team**, one member at a time, personal but not private, rotated through the practice.
8. **Tag a local business** from a genuine visit, as a Story, weekly.
9. **Anonymised case study carousel** - what they came in with, what they had tried, what changed, with written consent and modest claims.
10. **Real review turned into a Reel** over practice footage, never incentivised, never filtered.
11. **Office tour and "start here" Reels pinned permanently**, with parking and location details included.
12. **"Think you need to [common assumption]?"** followed by a specific number of alternatives.
13. **"After [N] years on [street]"** - your philosophy and what you disagree with, never naming a competitor.
14. **Stories poll** with a "just here for the results" option, weekly.
15. **"Micro habits I wish all my patients would adopt"** carousel, quarterly, with modest evidence-backed claims.

Formats 1, 3, 5, 7 and 11 are the ones a remote competitor cannot produce properly. Those are the ones to build the offer around.
